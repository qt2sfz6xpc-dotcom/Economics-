OCR A Level Economics exam technique is based on four assessment objectives: AO1 is knowledge and understanding, AO2 is application, AO3 is analysis, and AO4 is evaluation. This means that strong answers must not just define economic terms, but also apply them to the context of the question, develop clear chains of reasoning, and, in longer questions, reach a supported judgement. OCR rewards accurate economic theory, relevant use of data or examples, logical explanation, and balanced evaluation.

For 2 mark questions, the answer should be short and precise. These questions may ask for a definition, calculation, percentage change, elasticity value, or interpretation of data. Always show working for calculations and include units where needed. For example, if price rises from £20 to £25, the percentage change is 5 divided by 20 multiplied by 100, which equals 25%. For elasticity questions, use the correct formula and state whether the answer is elastic or inelastic if the question requires interpretation.

For 4 mark questions, use a clear point, data and explanation structure. The answer should usually make one or two developed points and use evidence from the information provided. Do not just quote the data; explain what the data shows economically. For example, if the data show that prices fall as the number of firms increases, explain that greater competition may increase supply and put pressure on firms to reduce prices in order to maintain market share.

For 8, 10 or 12 mark questions, OCR often expects a more developed answer. Start by defining the key term, then analyse one side of the argument, apply it to the context, and include a limitation or judgement if the command word asks you to evaluate. A strong answer should use phrases such as “this depends on”, “in the short run”, “in the long run”, “the significance of this depends on”, and “overall”. These questions are not just about remembering theory; they require you to use the information given in the question and explain why it matters.

For 15 mark questions, focus mainly on knowledge, application and analysis. This means defining key terms, using accurate economic theory, applying the answer to the context, and building logical chains of reasoning. Evaluation is only needed if the command word asks for it. Do not automatically follow AQA advice such as “no evaluation is needed in a 15 marker”; for OCR, you must pay close attention to the wording of the question. If the question says “explain” or “analyse”, focus on detailed explanation. If it says “evaluate”, include judgement. A good 15 marker should have a short introduction, two well-developed analytical paragraphs, relevant application, and a clear link back to the question.

For 25 mark questions, evaluation is essential. A strong OCR 25 marker should include definitions, application, analysis, diagrams where relevant, balanced evaluation and a final supported judgement. The basic structure should be: introduction, analysis paragraph, evaluation paragraph, second analysis paragraph, second evaluation paragraph, and conclusion. The introduction should define the key terms and give an overall line of argument. Each analysis paragraph should follow a clear chain of reasoning. Each evaluation paragraph should challenge the strength of the previous argument by considering assumptions, limitations or alternative outcomes. The conclusion should not simply repeat the essay; it should make a final judgement about which argument is strongest and why.

A strong OCR paragraph can follow the structure: define, apply, diagram if useful, explain, evaluate, judge. For example, if the question is about a tax on petrol cars, define indirect tax, apply it to petrol cars, explain that the tax raises firms’ costs and shifts supply left, causing price to rise and quantity demanded to fall, then evaluate by saying the effect depends on the price elasticity of demand. If demand is inelastic because consumers need cars for work and have few substitutes, the fall in quantity may be small, so pollution may not fall significantly.

For microeconomics essays, always focus on markets, incentives, demand and supply, elasticity, market failure, government intervention, costs, revenues, profits and efficiency. Useful diagrams include demand and supply, externalities, indirect taxes, subsidies, price controls, monopoly, perfect competition, and cost/revenue diagrams. Evaluation may include elasticity, time period, magnitude, government failure, information failure, unintended consequences, and whether the policy targets the root cause of the problem.

For macroeconomics essays, always link the answer to macroeconomic performance. This includes economic growth, inflation, unemployment, the current account, living standards, inequality, productivity and government finances. Useful diagrams include AD/AS, LRAS, Keynesian AS, Phillips curve, and exchange rate diagrams. Evaluation may include short run versus long run, spare capacity, confidence, interest elasticity, inflationary pressure, global economic conditions, import dependency, the multiplier, and policy conflicts.

Paper 1 is microeconomics. It focuses on markets, consumers, firms, market failure and government intervention. Paper 2 is macroeconomics. It focuses on national and international economic issues such as growth, inflation, unemployment, trade, fiscal policy, monetary policy and supply-side policy. Paper 3 is synoptic, meaning it combines microeconomics and macroeconomics. In Paper 3, you must be able to connect topics together. For example, a question on housing could involve micro ideas such as demand, supply, elasticity and market failure, but also macro ideas such as inflation, interest rates, inequality, labour mobility and economic growth.

The main correction from the original AQA-style notes is that OCR answers should be written around AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4. Do not use AQA-specific advice, AQA level names or AQA paper structures. Replace any statement such as “this would score Level 5 according to AQA” with OCR wording such as “this would reach a high level because it shows strong knowledge, application, analysis and supported evaluation.” Replace “you do not need evaluation in a 15 marker for AQA” with “for OCR, evaluation depends on the command word; explain and analyse questions need developed chains of reasoning, while evaluate questions need supported judgement.”

A strong OCR answer should always be precise, applied and logical. Weak answers make simple statements such as “subsidies lower prices and increase demand.” Strong answers explain the full chain: a subsidy lowers firms’ costs of production, shifts supply to the right, reduces price, increases quantity, and may increase consumption of a good with positive externalities. Strong evaluation then considers whether the subsidy is affordable, whether demand is price elastic, whether the government has enough information, and whether there may be opportunity costs.

A strong OCR conclusion should make a clear final judgement. It should say which argument or policy is most important, why it is most important, and what the judgement depends on. For example, instead of writing “overall, taxes are good,” write “overall, taxation is likely to be effective if demand is price elastic and the tax is set close to the external cost. However, where consumers have few substitutes, subsidies for alternatives such as public transport may be more effective in the long run. Therefore, a combination of taxation and subsidies is likely to be more effective than one policy alone.”
